# Understanding slide 12: successful edits, changed probabilities, and rare harm

Prepared for charlie by Capex, October 6, 2026. This explains slide 12, “Successful edits can still disturb other predictions,” in [long_deck_v1.pdf](../presentation-materials/deck_v4/long_deck_v1.pdf). It starts from the underlying ideas, then works through actual saved results. The small three-choice example is explicitly illustrative; the experimental numbers are identified separately.

**The slide's central point is that teaching the cap a requested answer can work while also changing predictions we did not intend to change.** To look for those changes, we replayed a fixed collection of ordinary text through the model with and without its cap. We examined both the complete set of next-token probabilities and the probability of the particular token that actually occurred in that text.

Those are two different measurements. Neither is simply the percentage of questions the model answered incorrectly.

## 1. What a language model predicts before it produces a word

A language model processes text as **tokens**: pieces of text that can be whole words, parts of words, punctuation, or other character sequences. A token may include a leading space. GPT-2's vocabulary in our main study contains **50,257 possible tokens**.

Before choosing the next token, the model assigns a probability to every token in that vocabulary. The probabilities add to one, or 100%. This complete list is its **next-token probability distribution**.

For illustration only, imagine a model with just three possible continuations after “The cup contains”:

| Possible next token | Without cap | With cap |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| water | 50% | 45% |
| coffee | 30% | 35% |
| juice | 20% | 20% |
| Total | 100% | 100% |

Both systems would choose “water” if they always selected the most likely token. Nevertheless, their distributions differ. The cap has moved probability from water to coffee.

This is why merely comparing generated answers can miss a change. A model can return the same visible answer while its probabilities have changed. Conversely, a small probability shift can change the visible answer when two candidates were almost tied.

The three-choice table is a teaching example, not a recorded experiment. Our measurements compared the full GPT-2 vocabulary. [Sources 1–2 below.]

## 2. What the cap can change when the transformer is frozen

“Frozen” means that the transformer's stored weights do not change during these edit streams. It does not mean that an attached cap cannot change the computation.

The cap retrieves a stored correction and adds numerical vectors to the transformer's temporary internal activations. Those altered activations can change the final next-token probabilities. If the cap applies no correction, the model gives its uncapped predictions for the same input.

We want a correction to apply when a question asks for a taught fact, including a differently worded version of that question. We generally do not want that correction to affect unrelated prose. A retrieval rule that generalizes can sometimes apply a correction where we did not intend it to. The resulting probability change may help the actual next token, hurt it, or leave that token unchanged while changing other possibilities.

Slide 12 measures these effects. It does not establish which stored fact caused every changed prediction. Nor does it mean the underlying GPT-2 weights were accidentally retrained. The [slide-10 explanation](slide-10-stable-v0-training-and-baselines.md) describes the learning stages and retrieval mechanisms in more detail.

## 3. What “ordinary text at identical prefixes” means

For this assay, the ordinary text came from the **WikiText-103 validation split**, using the fixed token file recorded in the project's data inventory. It was separate from the supplied factual-edit prompts. It was not a conversation generated by one of the experimental caps.

A **prefix** is the text preceding the token we want to predict. For each position, both systems received exactly the same prefix from the recorded text:

```text
Recorded prefix ──> frozen GPT-2 alone ──> next-token probabilities
       |
       └──────────> same GPT-2 + cap ────> next-token probabilities

Recorded next token ──> look up its probability in both distributions
```

We then extended the prefix using the next token from the recorded text and continued. We did not let one model generate its own continuation and then compare it with another model's different continuation. That would mix the effect of the cap with the effect of giving the models different inputs.

Supplying the actual preceding tokens is sometimes called **teacher forcing**. In this assay it was an evaluation procedure: no model weights or correction-memory contents were trained on those text tokens. Each scored prefix made a fresh retrieval decision; a decision from a previous prefix was not carried forward as if this were one freely generated answer.

### Where 245,237 comes from

The prepared validation file contains 247,289 tokens. The full assay used complete blocks, called **windows**, of 128 tokens:

- 1,931 complete windows contain 247,168 tokens.
- Each window supplies 127 next-token prediction positions: use its first token to predict its second, its first two to predict its third, and so on.
- Therefore, **1,931 × 127 = 245,237 scored positions**.
- The remaining 121 tokens were omitted under the declared incomplete-window rule.

Context restarted at each window. This is a measurement on that particular finite text inventory with that context convention, not an exhaustive test of ordinary language or long conversations. “Full validation” means all complete windows of this inventory; it does not mean every conceivable validation example.

These full assays were performed at the final edit checkpoint. A smaller 128-window sample was also used at checkpoints, but slide 12's number refers to the full final assay. [Sources 2–3.]

## 4. The right-hand question: did the actual next token become less likely?

Suppose the next token in a recorded passage is “water.” We look up the probability that each system assigned to that token. Call them `p_base` and `p_cap`.

The slide uses **Δ**, pronounced “delta,” to mean the change in prediction loss:

```text
Δ = ln(p_base / p_cap)
```

`ln` is the natural logarithm. It converts probability ratios into an additive scale. The units on that scale are **nats**. A nat is a unit of information or prediction loss, not a percentage or a count of wrong answers.

The sign has a straightforward interpretation:

| Situation | Ratio p_base / p_cap | Δ | Interpretation for the recorded token |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Its probability is unchanged | 1 | 0 | No change in this token's loss. |
| Its probability is halved | 2 | About +0.693 nat | It became less likely. |
| Its probability falls to one tenth | 10 | About +2.303 nats | It became much less likely. |
| Its probability doubles | 0.5 | About −0.693 nat | It became more likely. |

For example, changing a probability from 20% to 10% and changing it from 0.02% to 0.01% both give Δ ≈ +0.693. Their **relative** reduction is the same, although their changes in percentage points are very different.

In the illustrative water/coffee/juice table, if the recorded token were water, Δ would be `ln(0.50 / 0.45) ≈ +0.1054`. If it were coffee, Δ would be negative. If it were juice, Δ would be zero. The recorded text determines which one is actually scored.

### Why this is called loss or “harm”

The standard next-token loss is **negative log probability**, abbreviated **NLL**:

```text
NLL = −ln(probability assigned to the recorded token)
```

A model incurs more loss when it assigns less probability to what actually happened. Subtracting base loss from cap loss gives:

```text
Δ = NLL_cap − NLL_base
  = −ln(p_cap) + ln(p_base)
  = ln(p_base / p_cap)
```

In these reports, **harm is an operational term for a positive loss increase on a recorded text token**. It is not a direct measurement of injury to a person, factual misinformation, the severity of a generated sentence, or whether an answer would be useful. Negative Δ is an improvement in this prediction score, not proof that the cap has become generally better.

The observed text token is also not the factual edit's “new target.” It comes from the ordinary-text validation passage. That distinction matters: here we are testing a side effect of the taught edits. [Sources 2 and 4.]

## 5. The left-hand question: how much did the whole distribution change?

**KL** stands for **Kullback–Leibler divergence**. Here it compares the complete distribution of next-token probabilities from the frozen base with the distribution from the capped model.

One way to understand the calculation is:

1. Consider every possible next token, not just the one present in the text.
2. For each token, calculate its log probability ratio, `ln(p_base / p_cap)`.
3. Weight that value by how much probability the base assigned to that token.
4. Add the weighted values.

In mathematical notation, where `t` ranges over the vocabulary:

```text
KL(base ∥ cap) = Σ_t p_base(t) × ln[p_base(t) / p_cap(t)]
```

The double bar identifies the direction of the comparison. Reversing base and cap generally gives a different answer. The experiment uses **base as the reference**, in the direction shown above.

KL is zero when the distributions are identical and positive when they differ, apart from negligible numerical roundoff. It is not a signed “better or worse” score. Some individual terms in the sum can be negative, but their complete probability-weighted sum cannot be negative mathematically.

For the illustrative three-choice table, KL is approximately **0.006435 nats**. That value is the same whichever of the three tokens happens to occur in the recorded text. Δ, by contrast, depends on that observed token. In particular, if the observed token is juice, Δ is zero while KL is positive: probabilities of the other possibilities changed.

This gives the distinction in one table:

| Measurement | What does it use at one position? | Can it be negative? | What does it answer? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Δ | Probability of the one recorded next token | Yes | Did this recorded token become more or less likely? |
| KL | Probabilities of all possible next tokens | No, mathematically | How far did the prediction distribution move from the base? |

KL weights its possible-token average according to the base. The mean Δ measured on recorded prose uses the tokens that actually occur in that prose. Those are different averages, so there is no requirement that they agree.

### Fidelity is not the same as truth or usefulness

**Fidelity** here means staying close to the original model's behavior on this text assay. The original model is the reference system, not an oracle of correct answers.

A cap can improve the probability of the recorded token and nevertheless have positive KL because it changed the distribution. Conversely, staying exactly faithful to the base preserves the base's mistakes too. Fidelity is useful for asking whether a supposedly selective intervention is disturbing other behavior; it does not replace the tests of whether the requested edits work. [Sources 2 and 5.]

## 6. What “all 45 cells exceeded 0.001” actually says

A **cell** is one experimental run with a particular dataset, cap condition, subject realization and edit ordering. A subject realization is one selected collection of facts; an ordering specifies the sequence in which those facts were taught.

The 45 cells in this statement are only the main **learned-reader** condition:

```text
3 datasets × 3 subject realizations × 5 edit orders = 45 cells
```

Each cell had its own acquired correction memory. All reused the selected trained reader and the same frozen GPT-2 weights. They were not 45 independently trained neural networks. There were another 45 random-reader cells and another 45 stable-v0 cells in the primary triplet; those are outside this particular “all 45” claim.

For each learned-reader cell, the analysis averaged its 245,237 position-level KL values. The registered benchmark was:

```text
Mean KL ≤ 0.001 nat: pass this distribution-fidelity criterion.
Mean KL > 0.001 nat: fail this criterion.
```

Every one of the 45 learned-reader cell means exceeded the limit. The actual ranges were:

| Memory taught on | Cells | Final edit count per cell | Range of cell mean KL |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| zsRE | 15 | 1,000 | 0.002330–0.002448 |
| CounterFact | 15 | 1,000 | 0.004728–0.005781 |
| MQuAKE | 15 | 300 | 0.004092–0.007123 |

All three rows use the same ordinary-text validation inventory. The dataset label tells us which factual edits filled the memory before the text assay. It does not mean that each row was tested on a different ordinary-text corpus. MQuAKE's shorter editing run remains a descriptive comparison.

The limit was a chosen experimental standard. It is not a universal boundary separating safe models from unsafe models. Also, 0.001 is **not** “0.1% wrong answers,” “0.1% changed tokens,” or a bound on every individual position. It is a limit on an average in nats. Failing it says the system did not meet the promised degree of average distribution preservation on this assay.

### Three thresholds that are easy to confuse

| Number | Applied to | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.001 nat | **Mean KL** over all positions in a cell | Distribution-fidelity benchmark on slide 12. |
| 0.01 nat | **Mean signed Δ** over all positions in a cell | A separate average-loss fidelity benchmark in the report. |
| 0.01 nat | **An individual position's Δ** | Threshold for counting harmful changes in the tail analysis and slide 13. |

The last two happen to use the same number, but they ask different questions. All 45 learned-reader cells passed the mean-signed-Δ limit while failing the mean-KL limit. That is consistent: neither quantity is a substitute for the other, and a small mean Δ can conceal occasional large increases. [Sources 4–5.]

## 7. An actual saved run, worked through

Take the learned-reader **zsRE realization 0, order 100**, after 1,000 edits. This is the same run used for the Sporting Canamy example, rather than a run chosen for the worst overall harm. Its final retained-paraphrase score was **95.5%**.

The ordinary-text assay for that run recorded:

| Quantity | Actual saved value | Reading |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Scored positions | 245,237 | One fixed ordinary-text assay. |
| Positions with exactly zero Δ | 244,760, about 99.8055% | No recorded change in observed-token loss at those positions. |
| Positions with positive Δ | 401 | The recorded token became less likely. |
| Positions with negative Δ | 76 | The recorded token became more likely. |
| Positions with Δ > 0.01 nat | 395, about 0.1611% | Above the tail analysis's reporting threshold. |
| Mean Δ among those 395 positions | 1.606745 nats | Severity conditional on exceeding that threshold. |
| Mean signed Δ over all positions | 0.00245023 nat | Below the separate 0.01 average-loss limit. |
| Mean KL over all positions | 0.00238149 nat | About 2.38 times the 0.001 KL limit. |
| Largest Δ in this run | 9.262267 nats | A much larger individual change than the mean suggests. |

The 0.1611% frequency is for this one run. Slide 13's 0.1594% zsRE learned-reader frequency summarizes all fifteen such cells, so the two numbers are not meant to be identical.

### What the largest change meant in probability terms

The run's largest Δ occurred at saved location `w860:p44`—window index 860, target position 44 in the evaluator's indexing. The recorded next token was **` As`**, including a leading space. The saved losses imply these probabilities, calculated as `exp(−NLL)`:

| System | Probability assigned to that recorded token | NLL |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Frozen base without cap | About 0.0000294213, or 0.00294213% | 10.433792 |
| Same base with the learned cap | About 0.00000000279325, or 0.000000279325% | 19.696060 |

The difference is `19.696060 − 10.433792 ≈ 9.262267` nats. Equivalently, the cap made this already unlikely token about **10,533 times less likely**. The base was not confidently predicting it correctly in the first place. This is a large *relative probability reduction*, not evidence that a previously correct top-choice answer necessarily became wrong.

At this same position, KL was approximately **6.744336 nats**. That differs from Δ because KL accounts for the other 50,256 vocabulary entries as well. This was the position of maximum Δ in this run, not its maximum KL position.

These figures were recovered from the existing saved loss/KL array and its corresponding token inventory, with the array's recorded hash checked. No model was rerun to create this example. Across all 45 learned-reader cells, the largest observed Δ was **17.06053 nats**; 9.262267 is the maximum of the particular run explained here. [Sources 4 and 6.]

## 8. Why a small mean can conceal a large problem

A mean takes the sum and divides it by the number of positions. In the example above, over 244,000 exact zeros heavily dilute the contribution of the few hundred changed positions. Negative Δ values also offset some positive ones in the signed mean.

KL has no negative values to cancel its positive values, but its mean can still dilute a small number of large changes among many zeros. Therefore, this issue is not only about positive and negative changes cancelling.

There is an especially striking detail in the saved example: the **99th percentile of positive-part loss is zero**, while the worst loss increase is 9.262267 nats. Fewer than 1% of positions have positive harm, so a percentile at 99% can still land among the zeros. This is why the reports also examine the extreme observations rather than relying only on means or a single percentile.

Several summaries answer different questions:

- **Frequency:** how often does Δ exceed a stated size? The slide-13 threshold is 0.01 nat, which corresponds to roughly a 1% relative probability reduction.
- **Conditional severity:** among positions above that threshold, how large is Δ on average? Unchanged and improved positions are outside this average.
- **Maximum:** how large was the single worst observed Δ? This describes a saved observation, not a bound on future inputs.
- **Tail curve:** for successively larger thresholds, what fraction of all positions exceeds each one?
- **Expected shortfall:** average the worst specified fraction of positive-part losses. In this project, ES99+ uses the worst 1% of all positions after replacing negative losses with zero; it is not the mean over only the positive events.

A **tail** is the part of a distribution containing unusually large values. “Heavy-tailed” is a stronger mathematical claim about how slowly the probability of very large values decreases. Rare large observations motivate checking the tail, but do not by themselves prove a power law, infinite variance, or a particular heavy-tail family.

The project's HT-17 analysis found finite-range tail differences. Stable v0 on zsRE had a more pronounced observed tail than an exponential model predicted. For the learned reader, adding a generalized-Pareto shape parameter gave little additional predictive value on held-out windows. Those findings are narrower than declaring that all our learned-cap errors belong to a heavy-tailed universality class. The practical lesson of slide 12 remains: **measure the extremes as well as the average, whatever tail family is ultimately justified.** [Sources 4 and 6.]

## 9. Why passing data-integrity checks did not mean passing fidelity

These checks concern different things:

| Check | Question it addresses | What a pass does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data/artifact integrity | Are the recorded inputs, identities, output coverage and saved artifacts consistent with the declared run? | That the cap meets its scientific performance target. |
| Editing efficacy | Does the capped system produce the supplied answers on edited questions? | That unrelated predictions are preserved. |
| Distribution fidelity | Is the average KL change small enough under the chosen limit? | That every individual change is small, or that answers are true. |

An instrument can be working correctly and faithfully record that a treatment failed its target. Here the completed data support the conclusion that the learned reader did not meet the stated distribution-fidelity benchmark. Passing integrity checks is a reason to take that measured failure seriously, not a reason to override it.

Under the project's recorded decision, these cap-fidelity thresholds were **secondary benchmarks**, not automatic conditions for rejecting the run's data or stopping its execution. The failure therefore remains a scientific result to report alongside the successful edits. Integrity checks also do not certify that every possible implementation bug or methodological limitation has been ruled out. [Source 5.]

## 10. What the slide does and does not establish

The results show selective factual editing that generalizes to many paraphrases, together with measurable changes on ordinary text. Some of those changes sharply reduce the probability of a recorded token. They show that “good edit accuracy” and “preserves the base elsewhere” are separate requirements.

They do not establish how often a human would notice a harmful answer, how costly a real-world error would be, or performance on all future text. The 245,237 positions are not independent experiments: neighboring prefixes share text, positions share windows and articles, and every cell reuses the same validation inventory. The five edit orders within each realization also reuse facts. A large token count does not replace independent populations or justify treating all repeated cell-position measurements as independent evidence.

For the main three caps, the uncapped reference is the same original frozen GPT-2. Other reports involving continued-base controls distinguish a system's own cap-off base from the original model. The “all 45” statement here concerns the main learned-reader system, not those controls or the later predictive-coding-trained readers.

For the presentation's themes, slide 12 supplies the empirical motivation for studying extreme losses. The separate predictive-coding experiments ask whether changing the learning rule changes outcomes. Active inference motivates future choices about when to intervene or abstain based on expected consequences. This probability audit does not itself demonstrate an implemented active-inference policy or a coupled-entropy learning objective.

## A short explanation to say aloud with slide 12

“The cap can learn the new answers and still interfere with other predictions. We checked this by giving the capped and uncapped model exactly the same ordinary-text prefixes. A language model assigns probabilities to every possible next token. KL measures how much that whole distribution changes; delta measures whether the token actually present in the text becomes more or less likely. Most positions barely change, but a few changes are large. In one saved run, over 99.8% of positions had no loss change, yet one token became more than ten thousand times less likely. All 45 learned-reader runs exceeded our chosen average-KL limit. The data checks passed; the preservation target did not.”
